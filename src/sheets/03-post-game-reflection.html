Post-Game Reflection

The what-happened sheet

The moments right after a game are the cheapest lessons you will ever get — the position is still fresh, and your memory of the turning point is still reliable. Ten honest minutes on this sheet turns a game into a plan. Fill it in before you analyze with a computer, not after.

How to use it

  1. Cool down first, then write. A sip of water, a short walk. Feelings cool, facts stay.
  2. Name what decided it. The two “turning point” boxes are for the move (or moves) where the game swung — write the move number if you still remember it.
  3. Be kind on purpose. “Got outplayed” is allowed. “Lost it over the board” is also allowed. What is not useful is deciding you are bad at chess because of one hour.
  4. Pick exactly one drill for next time. One thing, written down, gets done. Five things get abandoned.
  5. Keep every sheet. Three months of them shows you the pattern no single game can — and the pattern is usually one specific weakness, not “everything.”
Pencils only. This sheet is for how the game felt while it was still true — erasing and revising after engine analysis is missing the point. Write, reflect, move on.
Beginner tip: The strongest players lose about as often as anyone at your level — the difference is what they feed back into training. A reflection sheet is how you score your own game instead of just the result.
